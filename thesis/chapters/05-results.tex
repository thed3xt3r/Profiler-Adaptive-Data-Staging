\section{Results}
\label{ch:results}
\addcontentsline{lot}{section}{Results}
%% ============================================================================

This chapter reports the evaluation described in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation-plan}, across all three architectures --
DeepLabV3+, MA-Net and SegFormer -- unless stated otherwise.
Section~\ref{sec:res-rq1} establishes when the loader is the bottleneck (RQ1);
Sections~\ref{sec:res-rq2}--\ref{sec:res-rq4} address sharding, predictive staging
and prefetch-depth auto-tuning in turn.
Section~\ref{sec:res-accuracy} verifies the accuracy constraint, and
Section~\ref{sec:res-profiler-bias} reports a measurement defect discovered while
interpreting the RQ4 and Section~\ref{ch:ablation} results, since it changes how
several of the numbers below should be read.

Every figure in this chapter and in Section~\ref{ch:ablation} is a single run
($n=1$), not a mean over repeats. The experimental protocol
(Section~\ref{ch:methodology}) calls for repeating each configuration and
reporting mean $\pm$ standard deviation, since measurements on a shared
filesystem are non-stationary and a single run is not strong evidence on its
own; cluster time did not permit this for the 91-configuration sweep reported
here, and Section~\ref{ch:futurework} discusses the consequence directly rather
than leaving it implicit in the absence of error bars. The comparisons below
should be read as a single, reasonably comprehensive pass across architectures
and configurations, not as statistically established differences.

The prediction under test was directional and stated in advance: SegFormer, having
the lightest forward pass of the three architectures, should be the configuration
in which the loader bottleneck appears first and persists most severely, because a
model that computes quickly spends proportionally more of its step waiting on data.
DeepLabV3+ and MA-Net, heavier, should tolerate the loader better.
The ordering in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-loader-util} does \emph{not} hold cleanly in
this direction: SegFormer's tuned utilisation (40.5\%) is the lowest of the three,
consistent with the prediction, but MA-Net's default utilisation (34.4\%) is not
meaningfully different from DeepLabV3+'s (33.6\%), despite MA-Net's decoder being
measurably the heaviest of the three architectures under every other measurement in
this thesis (Table~\ref{tab:rq1-breakdown}, and again in
Section~\ref{sec:res-rq4}'s fixed-depth sweep, where MA-Net alone saturates near
80\% GPU utilisation regardless of staging depth). The prediction's direction is
therefore only partially confirmed: it holds for the lightest architecture but does
not order the two heavier ones as expected. This is reported as stated in advance,
because the framing of this thesis commits to reporting a failed directional
prediction rather than silently dropping it.

\Tdata{} dominates \Tgpu{} by a wide margin under the default configuration across
all three architectures -- 8--20$\times$, using the corrected measurement of
Section~\ref{sec:res-profiler-bias} rather than the uncorrected 8-batch probe, which
understated the ratio by roughly an order of magnitude for reasons given there.
\Tdecode{} and \Thtod{} are both negligible throughout: \Thtod{} never exceeds one
millisecond in any run in this thesis, confirming it is not a plausible bottleneck
for this workload regardless of architecture or policy.
This is the load-bearing evidence for the rest of the thesis: training on this
corpus, on this filesystem, is I/O-bound rather than compute-bound by a wide margin,
which is what motivates a data-staging system as the correct intervention.

\subsection{RQ2: Do tar shards reduce many-small-file overhead?}
\label{sec:res-rq2}

The direct measure is filesystem operation counts, not wall-clock: sharding is
intended to convert many small metadata operations into few large sequential
reads, and that intent is verifiable independently of whether it happens to pay off
in this configuration.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Policy & \code{open} calls/batch & \code{stat} calls/batch & Bytes read/batch \\
\midrule
loose (measured at $\gamma_d=1.5$) & 63.7 & 31.8 & 5{,}450{,}413 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[Filesystem operations per batch, loose policy]{Filesystem operations issued against GPFS per batch (batch size 32,
\code{num\_workers=0}, consistent across all three architectures since the reader
and dataset composition are identical). Sharded and staged configurations were not
independently instrumented for fs-op counts at this granularity in this session's
runs; the profiler counts operations only for the probed (loose) reader, since
\code{profile\_data\_pipeline} always profiles a loose probe before a policy is
selected (Section~\ref{ch:methodology}).}
\label{tab:rq2-fsops}
\end{table}

At roughly 64 \code{open} and 32 \code{stat} calls per 32-item batch -- two
filesystem operations per sample, one for the image and one for the mask -- every
sample under \code{loose} incurs its own metadata round-trip to the shared
filesystem. This is the mechanistic explanation for RQ1's finding: \Tdata{}'s
dominance over \Tgpu{} is not primarily a bandwidth problem (5.45\,MB/batch is
trivial for GPFS) but a metadata-operation-count problem, which is exactly the
pathology sharding is designed to amortise by consolidating many small files into
few large sequential archives.

Two comparisons carry the argument, and both resolve against \pads{} on two of

Against method~4, the full pre-stage ceiling: \pads{} matches or exceeds it only

The reason, established in Section~\ref{sec:res-profiler-bias} below, is that

staging \emph{does} help, sometimes substantially (SegFormer: 52.8\% vs.\ 39.5--40.5\%
for the two non-staged methods). It is evidence that the adaptive decision layer,
as measured, is too conservative to capture a benefit the underlying mechanism
demonstrably has. Section~\ref{sec:res-profiler-bias} traces this to a specific,
quantified measurement defect in the profiler rather than leaving it as an
unexplained shortfall.

The claim under test was falsifiable in a specific way: the auto-tuned depth
should land at or near the best fixed depth without having been told it.
It does not. Every auto-tuned depth in Table~\ref{tab:rq4-auto} lands at
$d \approx 170$--$235$ -- one to two orders of magnitude outside the $1$--$8$
range the fixed sweep tests, and where a fixed depth was actually shown to help
(Table~\ref{tab:rq4-depth}, $d=4$ for two of three architectures). The derivation
in Section~\ref{ch:methodology} is not simply imprecise here; it is measuring a
quantity, $T_{stage}$, that is unstable by three to four orders of magnitude
between repeated measurements on the same hardware and dataset. SegFormer's own
$d=8$ fixed-depth run independently stalled for 66 minutes on a filesystem hiccup
during its first attempt before completing cleanly on retry -- the same underlying
phenomenon (shared-filesystem contention distorting a staging-time measurement)
surfacing as a training-time failure rather than only a profiling anomaly.

Section~\ref{ch:methodology}'s own framing anticipates $T_{stage}$ jitter on a
shared filesystem -- it is the stated reason the formula carries a ``+1'' slack
term -- but not jitter at the magnitude observed here. The auto-tuned depth does
not bracket with the fixed sweep the way the methodology's framing expects, and
this is reported as a specific, quantified limitation of the depth-derivation
formula rather than glossed over.

\subsection{Why the adaptive layer stayed conservative: a profiler measurement defect}
\label{sec:res-profiler-bias}

RQ3 and RQ4 both surfaced the same pattern: \pads{}'s adaptive profiler almost
never selected staging, even where a fixed configuration demonstrably benefited
from it. This section traces that pattern to its cause rather than leaving it as
an unexplained shortfall in the adaptive design.

\pads{}'s policy decision is driven by a single warm-up probe: by default, 8
batches ($\approx$12--15\,s wall-clock), read in a fixed, unshuffled order, at the
very start of a run. To test whether this window is representative, the same
probe was re-run at \code{--profile\_batches 147} -- a full epoch -- instead of
the default 8, for DeepLabV3+ and SegFormer.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}
\toprule
Run & \Tdata{} (s) & \Tgpu{} (s) & Ratio & Policy chosen \\
\midrule
DeepLabV3+, 8-batch (default)        & 0.783 & 0.613 & 1.28 & loose \\
DeepLabV3+, 147-batch (full epoch)   & 0.808 & \textbf{0.100} & \textbf{8.07} & shard \\
SegFormer, 8-batch (default)         & 0.752 & 0.655 & 1.15 & loose \\
SegFormer, 147-batch (full epoch)    & 0.841 & \textbf{0.062} & \textbf{13.5} & shard \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[8-batch probe vs.\ full-epoch measurement]{8-batch probe vs.\ full-epoch measurement, same architecture and
configuration. \Tdata{} barely moves; the entire distortion is on \Tgpu{}, which
drops 6--10$\times$ once measured over a full epoch instead of 8 batches.}
\label{tab:profiler-bias-raw}
\end{table}

The mechanism is a known effect, and -- this is worth stating precisely rather
than presenting the fix as a new idea -- one the design already anticipated in
writing without the implementation actually applying it.
Section~\ref{ch:methodology}'s aggregation step specifies exactly this failure
mode: ``the first batches of a run include CUDA context creation, cuDNN
algorithm selection and worker process start-up, all of which are one-off costs
that would inflate a mean and bias the classifier,'' and prescribes discarding
the first $w_0=10$ batches and taking the \emph{median} rather than the mean
over the remainder for precisely this reason. The shipped
\code{profile\_data\_pipeline()} implemented neither safeguard: it discarded no
batches and computed a plain mean over all 8. Table~\ref{tab:profiler-bias-raw}
is that gap made visible -- the one-time cuDNN kernel autotuning and CUDA
allocator warm-up cost described in Section~\ref{ch:methodology} dominates a
mean of only 8 batches and makes \Tgpu{} look far larger than its steady-state
value; averaged over 147, it is diluted down to close to the truth. An inflated
\Tgpu{} shrinks the \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio directly, which is exactly what was
making nearly every Section~\ref{ch:ablation} run look balanced enough to
remain on \code{loose}.

This diagnostic tests and confirms one of the two documented safeguards, not
both. The discard mechanism was implemented and swept (below); the specified
median aggregation was not implemented or tested here, and remains an open
question -- Section~\ref{sec:defects-session2} of Appendix~\ref{app:defects}
records this explicitly as unfinished rather than folding it into the ``fixed''
claim below.

A 147-batch probe fixes the bias but is not a practical default
(Table~\ref{tab:overhead}: $\approx$130\,s of overhead per run, against 12--15\,s
for the 8-batch default). The follow-up question is therefore not how large the
window must be, but whether the fix is a larger window at all, or simply
excluding the warm-up batches from whatever window is used.
\code{profile\_data\_pipeline()} was extended with a \code{discard\_batches}
parameter -- run $N$ batches unmeasured before the timed loop starts -- and a
\code{checkpoint\_batches} parameter that snapshots the running ratio at several
post-discard batch counts within one pass, avoiding a separate full re-run per
candidate window size.

All three architectures land within 3\% of the full-epoch ground truth using only
8 post-discard batches, and adding more (16/32/64) barely moves the number
further -- the error does not shrink monotonically with window size either
(DeepLabV3+'s 64-batch reading is closer than its 32-batch one), consistent with
the residual 1--3\% being ordinary sampling noise rather than remaining warm-up
bias. This reframes the defect precisely: it is not a window-size problem, it is
a warm-up-contamination problem, exactly as Section~\ref{ch:methodology}
already described. Discarding the first 5 batches, then measuring only 8 more
(13 total, essentially the current cost), recovers the accuracy of a 147-batch
probe at a fraction of its overhead -- with fewer discarded batches than the
$w_0=10$ originally specified, though this diagnostic swept the
\emph{measurement} window (8/16/32/64 batches) at a single fixed discard count
rather than sweeping the discard count itself, so ``5 suffices'' should be read
as ``5 was sufficient in this test,'' not as a claim that discard values
between 5 and 10 were compared and found unnecessary. \textbf{The corrected
profiler default is \code{--sweep\_discard 5 --profile\_batches 8}} in place of
the shipped \code{--profile\_batches 8} with no discard; both flags are
implemented in all three \code{3-pads/*/train.py}, bringing the implementation
into line with Section~\ref{ch:methodology}'s discard-based safeguard, though
not yet with its median-aggregation one (Appendix~\ref{app:defects},
Section~\ref{sec:defects-session2}). Measured fairly, every full-window ratio
in Table~\ref{tab:profiler-bias-sweep} (10.3--20.2$\times$) comfortably clears
even the most aggressive threshold tested in Section~\ref{ch:ablation}
($\gamma_d=1.0$), which is exactly why the adaptive policy almost never left
\code{loose} under the old, unimplemented-safeguard measurement.

This is not an isolated quirk of this cluster. TensorFlow's \code{tf.data}
\code{AUTOTUNE}, the closest well-known analogue to what this profiler attempts,
runs a background thread continuously throughout training, using gradient descent
to retune parallelism and buffer sizes rather than committing to a decision from
one early snapshot~\cite{Murray21}. Fixed-window sampling more generally is known
to either fire too early, capturing an unstable transient, or too late; recent
work on kernel-based steady-state detection for performance time series makes
this case directly and proposes continuous monitoring with a statistical
stability test in place of a fixed sample count~\cite{Beseda25}. The
discard-then-measure correction validated here is the minimal version of that
same idea. Shared-filesystem I/O interference from unrelated workloads is
independently documented as a dominant source of variance for exactly the
many-small-reads access pattern a DataLoader produces~\cite{Coles26}, which is
consistent with -- and does not fully explain, since $T_{gpu}$ rather than
$T_{data}$ was the biased quantity here -- the broader instability this thesis
observes on this filesystem.

\subsection{Accuracy is preserved}
\label{sec:res-accuracy}

A data-path optimisation is only sound if it leaves the model's output
unchanged. Sharding, staging and prefetching each alter how samples reach the
trainer -- their storage format, their ordering, and when they are materialised
-- so any of them could in principle corrupt, duplicate or stale-serve data, and
a throughput gain bought that way would be worthless. Segmentation quality is
therefore treated throughout this thesis as a constraint to be checked, not as
an objective to be advanced, and it is reported outside the throughput
narrative to keep the two claims separate.

The check is negative, which is the intended result: across the full
component-ablation sweep of Section~\ref{sec:ablation-components}, no policy
produces a consistent accuracy deviation from \code{loose} in either direction
across all three architectures, with a spread of at most $\pm2.5$ percentage
points within an architecture. Neither the sharded reader nor the staging path
trades accuracy for the throughput reported in this chapter. The supporting
per-policy figures are given in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction},
Table~\ref{tab:accuracy-guardrail}, together with the separate question of
whether these models reach the reference study's published accuracy at all --
which Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction} answers directly, and which is a
reproduction result rather than a claim about \pads{}.
