\subsection{RQ1: When does the DataLoader become the bottleneck?}
\label{sec:res-rq1}

\label{tab:rq1-loader-util}
\end{table}

The prediction under test was directional and stated in advance: architectures with lower per-step compute time should expose the DataLoader bottleneck more strongly because less GPU computation is available to overlap with data loading. In other words, for a fixed data-loading delay, a model with a short forward and backward pass spends a larger proportion of each training step waiting for data than a model with a longer computation time. SegFormer was therefore expected to be the configuration in which the loader bottleneck appeared most clearly, since its measured per-step GPU compute time of $0.031$~s is approximately half that of MA-Net ($0.062$~s) and DeepLabV3+ ($0.065$~s).

The results in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-loader-util} are consistent with this prediction. SegFormer records the lowest tuned GPU utilisation at 40.5\%, indicating that its comparatively short computation time leaves less opportunity to hide data-loading latency behind GPU execution. In contrast, MA-Net and DeepLabV3+ have substantially longer and nearly identical per-step compute times, differing by less than 5\%. Their similar default GPU utilisations of 34.4\% and 33.6\%, respectively, are therefore not evidence against the prediction; rather, the hypothesis provides little reason to expect a meaningful ordering between two architectures whose measured compute times are so close.

The relevant separation is consequently between SegFormer and the two heavier-compute architectures, rather than between MA-Net and DeepLabV3+. SegFormer's per-step compute time is approximately half that of either CNN architecture, making the same data-loading delay proportionally more significant within each training step. The observed results therefore support the predicted relationship between per-step compute time and sensitivity to the DataLoader bottleneck: faster model computation exposes data-pipeline latency more readily, whereas longer computation provides a larger window in which data loading can be overlapped with GPU execution.

This is the load-bearing evidence for the rest of the thesis: training on this
corpus, on this filesystem, is I/O-bound rather than compute-bound by a wide margin,
which is what motivates a data-staging system as the correct intervention.

\subsection{RQ2: Do tar shards reduce many-small-file overhead?}
\label{sec:res-rq2}

The direct measure is filesystem operation counts, not wall-clock: sharding is
intended to convert many small metadata operations into few large sequential
reads, and that intent is verifiable independently of whether it happens to pay off
in this configuration.

\midrule
loose (at $\gamma_d=1.5$) & 63.7 & 31.8 & 5{,}450{,}413 \\
\bottomrule

\label{tab:rq2-fsops}

At roughly 64 \code{open} and 32 \code{stat} calls per 32-item batch -- two
filesystem operations per sample, one for the image and one for the mask -- every
sample under \code{loose} incurs its own metadata round-trip to the shared
filesystem. This is the explanation for RQ1's finding: \Tdata{}'s
dominance over \Tgpu{} is not primarily a bandwidth problem (5.45\,MB/batch is
trivial for GPFS) but a metadata-operation-count problem, which is exactly the
pathology sharding is designed to amortise by consolidating many small files into
few large sequential archives.

Two comparisons are important for evaluating \pads{}, and both show that its adaptive configuration does not outperform the corresponding alternatives consistently across the three architectures.

First, \pads{} is compared against method~4, which represents the full pre-staging ceiling by placing the required data on node-local scratch before training begins. \pads{} matches or slightly exceeds this ceiling only for MA-Net, reaching 84.8% GPU utilisation compared with 83.3% for method~4. For DeepLabV3+ and SegFormer, however, \pads{} falls short of the pre-staged configuration by 4.7 and 13.3 percentage points, respectively. The difference is particularly pronounced for SegFormer, where method~4 reaches 52.8% utilisation compared with 39.5--40.5% for the non-staged configurations. This is consistent with the earlier observation that SegFormer has substantially lower per-step compute time than the other two architectures and therefore has less computation available to overlap with data-loading delays.

Second, \pads{} is compared against method~2, the tuned baseline reported in Table~\ref{tab:rq1-loader-util}. Here, \pads{} again provides no decisive improvement. For DeepLabV3+, \pads{} reaches 79.5% utilisation compared with 82.6% for the tuned baseline, and therefore performs slightly worse. For MA-Net, the corresponding values are 84.8% and 85.1%, making the two configurations effectively equivalent. Taken together, these comparisons show that the adaptive \pads{} configuration does not provide a consistent utilisation improvement over either the tuned baseline or the full pre-staging ceiling in these runs.

The reason for this result is established in Section~\ref{sec:res-profiler-bias}. The adaptive profiler selected \code{loose} for all three method-5 runs reported in this table. Consequently, \pads{} did not activate its staging mechanism in any of these experiments. In practice, these runs therefore behave similarly to the tuned baseline: samples are read as individual loose files using a worker configuration comparable to the tuned baseline.

This result should therefore be interpreted as a limitation of the adaptive decision layer rather than evidence that staging itself is ineffective. Method~4 provides an empirical ceiling for the benefit of staging and shows that moving the dataset to node-local scratch can improve GPU utilisation, with the largest improvement occurring for SegFormer. This result is particularly relevant because SegFormer has the shortest measured per-step compute time and is therefore the architecture for which data-loading latency is expected to be most exposed. The underlying staging mechanism can thus provide a measurable benefit, but \pads{} fails to capture that benefit in these runs because its profiler does not select the staging policy. Section~\ref{sec:res-profiler-bias} attributes this behaviour to a specific and quantified measurement bias in the profiler, rather than treating the observed shortfall as an unexplained failure of the staging mechanism itself.

The fixed-depth sweep in Table~\ref{tab:rq4-depth} shows that increasing prefetch depth does not necessarily improve GPU utilisation. DeepLabV3+ increases from 50.3\% at $d=1$ to a maximum of 65.9\% at $d=4$, before falling to 47.3\% at $d=8$. SegFormer follows a similar pattern, increasing from 25.1\% at $d=1$ to 48.0\% at $d=4$, before declining to 39.4\% at $d=8$. MA-Net, by contrast, already reaches 82.6\% utilisation at $d=1$ and remains relatively insensitive to further increases in depth. The results therefore indicate that deeper prefetching is beneficial only up to the point at which the transfer latency is sufficiently hidden; increasing the depth beyond this point does not provide an additional utilisation benefit.

\label{tab:rq4-auto}
\end{table}

The claim under test is directly falsifiable: the auto-tuned procedure should select a prefetch depth at or near the best-performing fixed depth without being given that value in advance. The results in Table~\ref{tab:rq4-auto} do not support this claim. With the exception of DeepLabV3+'s anomalous second measurement, the automatically selected depths lie between approximately $d=170$ and $d=235$. These values are one to two orders of magnitude larger than the $d=1$--$8$ range evaluated in the fixed-depth sweep. More importantly, Table~\ref{tab:rq4-depth} shows that $d=4$ already provides the highest observed utilisation for DeepLabV3+ and SegFormer. The auto-tuning procedure therefore does not converge towards the region in which the fixed-depth experiments demonstrate that useful values lie.

The source of this discrepancy is the staging-time measurement, $T_{stage}$, used by the depth-derivation formula in Section~\ref{ch:methodology}. The measurements in Table~\ref{tab:rq4-auto} show that $T_{stage}$ is not sufficiently stable under the observed shared-filesystem conditions. Most strikingly, two DeepLabV3+ measurements taken minutes apart on identical hardware report staging times of 5.47\,s and 1.5\,ms, a difference of approximately 3,600$\times$. Since the calculated prefetch depth depends directly on the ratio $T_{stage}/T_{consume}$, this variation propagates directly into $d$: the two measurements produce auto-tuned depths of 235 and 2, respectively. The problem is therefore not simply that the formula estimates the optimal depth imprecisely; one of its primary inputs can vary by several orders of magnitude between repeated measurements.

The fixed-depth experiments provide independent evidence of the same shared-filesystem variability. SegFormer's first $d=8$ run stalled for 66 minutes because of a filesystem delay before completing normally when repeated. This represents the same underlying phenomenon appearing in a different part of the experiment: contention on the shared filesystem can cause an individual staging operation to take substantially longer than its typical duration. During auto-tuning, such an event can distort $T_{stage}$ and therefore the calculated prefetch depth; during training, it instead appears as an abnormally long-running or stalled experiment.

Section~\ref{ch:methodology} already anticipates variability in $T_{stage}$ on a shared filesystem, which is the stated reason for including the additional ``+1'' shard as a safety margin in the depth formula. However, this mechanism assumes comparatively small fluctuations in staging time and cannot compensate for the three-to-four-order-of-magnitude variation observed here. The automatically derived depths therefore do not fall within, or consistently bracket, the useful region identified by the fixed-depth sweep. This result is reported as a specific and quantified limitation of the current depth-derivation formula: its effectiveness depends on a sufficiently stable estimate of $T_{stage}$, an assumption that does not hold reliably under the shared-filesystem conditions observed in these experiments.

\subsection{Why the adaptive layer stayed conservative: a profiler measurement defect}
\label{sec:res-profiler-bias}

The results from RQ3 and RQ4 reveal the same behaviour: \pads{}'s adaptive profiler almost never selected staging, even in configurations where the fixed-policy experiments demonstrated that staging could improve performance. This section investigates the cause of that behaviour and identifies a measurement defect in the profiler rather than treating the conservative policy selection as an unexplained limitation of the adaptive design.

\pads{}'s policy decision is driven by a single warm-up probe at the beginning of each run. By default, this probe measures 8 batches, corresponding to approximately 12--15\,s of wall-clock time, in a fixed and unshuffled order. To determine whether this short initial window accurately represents steady-state training behaviour, the same profiling procedure was repeated using \code{--profile\_batches 147}, corresponding to a complete epoch, for all three architectures.

\centering
\begin{tabular}{lcccc}

\midrule
DeepLabV3+, 8-batch (default) & 0.783 & 0.613 & 1.28 & loose \\
DeepLabV3+, 147-batch (full epoch) & 0.808 & \textbf{0.100} & \textbf{8.07} & shard \\
MA-Net, 8-batch (default) & 0.873 & 0.713 & 1.22 & loose \\
MA-Net, 147-batch (full epoch) & 0.874 & \textbf{0.093} & \textbf{9.41} & shard \\
SegFormer, 8-batch (default) & 0.752 & 0.655 & 1.15 & loose \\
SegFormer, 147-batch (full epoch) & 0.841 & \textbf{0.062} & \textbf{13.5} & shard \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption[8-batch probe vs.\ full-epoch measurement]{Comparison between the original 8-batch probe and a full-epoch measurement for the same architecture and configuration. \Tdata{} remains approximately unchanged, whereas \Tgpu{} decreases by 6--10$\times$ when measured over a complete epoch rather than only the first 8 batches.}
\label{tab:profiler-bias-raw}
\end{table}

Table~\ref{tab:profiler-bias-raw} shows that increasing the profiling window has little effect on \Tdata{}. The major change occurs in \Tgpu{}, which decreases by approximately 6--10$\times$ when measured over a complete epoch. Consequently, the \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio increases from approximately 1.15--1.28 under the original 8-batch probe to 8.07--13.5 over the full epoch. This change is sufficiently large to alter the resulting policy decision from \code{loose} to \code{shard} for all three architectures.

The cause of this distortion is a warm-up effect that was already anticipated in the methodology. Section~\ref{ch:methodology} explicitly identifies the first batches of a training run as potentially unrepresentative because they include one-time costs such as CUDA context creation, cuDNN algorithm selection, worker-process start-up, and CUDA allocator warm-up. These costs do not represent steady-state training and can therefore inflate the measured GPU time when included in a short profiling window. The methodology consequently specifies two safeguards: discarding the first $w_0=10$ batches and calculating the \emph{*median*} over the remaining measurements rather than their mean.

The shipped implementation of \code{profile\_data\_pipeline()}, however, applied neither safeguard. It discarded no initial batches and calculated a plain mean over all 8 measured batches. Because the profiling window was short, the one-time initialisation costs contributed disproportionately to the resulting \Tgpu{} estimate. Table~\ref{tab:profiler-bias-raw} makes the resulting bias visible: when these initial costs are diluted across a complete epoch, \Tgpu{} approaches its steady-state value while \Tdata{} remains largely unchanged. The inflated \Tgpu{} produced by the original profiler therefore suppresses the \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio, making the pipeline appear substantially more balanced than it is during steady-state training. This explains why nearly every run in Section~\ref{ch:ablation} remained on the conservative \code{loose} policy.

The full-epoch diagnostic confirms the importance of one of the two safeguards specified in Section~\ref{ch:methodology}, but it does not test both. The discard mechanism was subsequently implemented and evaluated below. The specified median aggregation was not implemented or experimentally tested and therefore remains an open issue. Section~\ref{sec:defects-session2} of Appendix~\ref{app:defects} records this explicitly rather than including it within the corrected-profiler claim.

Although a 147-batch probe substantially reduces the warm-up bias, it is not a practical default profiling strategy. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:overhead}, the full-epoch probe requires approximately 130\,s, compared with only 12--15\,s for the original 8-batch probe. The relevant question is therefore not simply how large the profiling window should be, but whether a larger measurement window is required at all. An alternative is to exclude the initial warm-up batches and retain a short measurement window once the system has entered a representative steady-state regime.

To test this, \code{profile\_data\_pipeline()} was extended with a \code{discard\_batches} parameter, which processes $N$ batches without measurement before beginning the timed profiling loop. A \code{checkpoint\_batches} parameter was also introduced to record the running \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio at several post-discard window sizes within a single run. This allows multiple candidate measurement-window sizes to be evaluated without requiring a separate complete experiment for each window size.

The results in Table~\ref{tab:profiler-bias-sweep} show that a long measurement window is not required once the initial warm-up batches are excluded. After discarding the first 5 batches, all three architectures produce a \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio within 3\% of their respective full-epoch measurements using only 8 additional measured batches. Increasing the measurement window to 16, 32, or 64 batches produces little further improvement. The remaining error also does not decrease monotonically with increasing window size. For example, DeepLabV3+'s 64-batch measurement is closer to its full-epoch value than its 32-batch measurement. This behaviour is consistent with the remaining 1--3\% difference being ordinary sampling variation rather than residual warm-up bias.

The defect can therefore be characterised more precisely as a \emph{*warm-up contamination problem*}, rather than a profiling-window-size problem, consistent with the failure mode already described in Section~\ref{ch:methodology}. In these experiments, discarding the first 5 batches and measuring the following 8 requires only 13 batches in total while recovering approximately the same \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio as the 143-batch full-epoch probe. The corrected procedure therefore achieves measurement accuracy close to the full-epoch diagnostic at a fraction of its wall-clock overhead.

This result should not, however, be interpreted as demonstrating that 5 discarded batches are universally optimal. The experiment fixes the discard count at 5 and varies only the number of subsequently measured batches (8, 16, 32, and 64). It therefore demonstrates that 5 discarded batches were sufficient in this experiment, rather than establishing that discard counts between 5 and the originally specified $w_0=10$ are unnecessary.

The corrected profiler default is \code{--sweep\_discard 5 --profile\_batches 8}, replacing the shipped \code{--profile\_batches 8} configuration with no discarded batches. Both parameters are implemented in all three \code{3-pads/*/train.py} scripts. This brings the implementation into line with the discard-based safeguard specified in Section~\ref{ch:methodology}, although the median-aggregation safeguard remains unimplemented, as documented in Appendix~\ref{app:defects}, Section~\ref{sec:defects-session2}.

With the corrected measurement procedure, every full-window ratio in Table~\ref{tab:profiler-bias-sweep} lies between 10.3 and 20.2$\times$, comfortably exceeding even the most aggressive threshold evaluated in Section~\ref{ch:ablation}, $\gamma_d=1.0$. The original profiler's failure to discard warm-up batches therefore provides a direct explanation for why the adaptive policy almost never left \code{loose}: the early one-time GPU costs inflated \Tgpu{}, suppressed the \Tdata{}/\Tgpu{} ratio, and caused the decision layer to underestimate the severity of the data-loading bottleneck.

This limitation is not unique to the experimental environment considered here. TensorFlow's \code{tf.data} \code{AUTOTUNE}, a well-known analogue to the adaptive profiling approach used by \pads{}, continuously monitors training in a background thread and adjusts parallelism and buffer sizes throughout execution rather than committing to a configuration from a single early measurement~\cite{Murray21}. More generally, fixed-window performance measurements can either occur too early and capture transient behaviour or continue unnecessarily after the system has already stabilised. Recent work on kernel-based steady-state detection addresses this problem by replacing a fixed sample count with continuous monitoring and a statistical stability criterion~\cite{Beseda25}. The discard-then-measure correction evaluated here represents a simpler implementation of the same general principle: measurements used for adaptation should be taken only after the system has moved beyond its initial transient state.

Shared-filesystem interference provides an additional source of runtime variability. Contention from unrelated workloads has been documented as an important source of variance for the many-small-read access pattern produced by a DataLoader~\cite{Coles26}. This is consistent with the broader filesystem variability observed in this thesis, although it does not directly explain the profiler defect identified here, since the dominant measurement bias in this experiment occurs in $T_{gpu}$ rather than $T_{data}$.

\subsection{Accuracy is preserved}
\label{sec:res-accuracy}

A data-path optimisation is only valid if it preserves the model's output. Sharding, staging and prefetching alter how samples reach the trainer, including their storage representation, access order, and materialisation time. An implementation error in any of these mechanisms could therefore corrupt samples, duplicate them, omit them, or serve stale data. A throughput improvement obtained at the expense of model correctness would not constitute a valid optimisation. Segmentation quality is therefore treated throughout this thesis as a constraint that must be preserved rather than an objective that \pads{} attempts to improve, keeping the accuracy and throughput claims separate.

The expected result is therefore the absence of a systematic accuracy change. Across the full component-ablation sweep in Section~\ref{sec:ablation-components}, no policy produces a consistent accuracy deviation from \code{loose} in either direction across all three architectures. The observed spread remains within $\pm2.5$ percentage points for each architecture. There is consequently no evidence that either the sharded reader or the staging path obtains the throughput improvements reported in this chapter by sacrificing segmentation accuracy.

The supporting per-policy measurements are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}, Table~\ref{tab:accuracy-guardrail}. Whether the three reproduced models reach the accuracy reported by the original reference study is treated separately in Appendix~\ref{app:reproduction}.
