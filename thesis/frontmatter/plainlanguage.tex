\section*{Plain Language Summary}

Semantic segmentation -- assigning a label to every pixel of an image rather
than to the image as a whole -- has become a practical tool in archaeology.
This thesis works with \emph{tells}: mounds in southern Iraq built up from the
accumulated debris of settlements occupied over long periods, and distinctive
enough in satellite imagery that a neural network can be trained to trace their
outlines. There are thousands of them across the survey region, so an automated
first pass is worth a great deal to archaeologists deciding where to spend
limited excavation effort.

Training such a network on a computing cluster is conventionally treated as a
problem of keeping the GPU busy: enable mixed precision, raise the batch size,
optimise the model. This thesis measures where the time actually goes, and the
GPU turns out not to be the constraint. The training set is roughly ten thousand
small files on a parallel filesystem shared with every other job on the cluster.
Loading one sample means a separate open, stat, read and close for the image and
again for its mask, and the cost of those metadata operations is close to
constant no matter how few bytes are wanted. Measured across three architectures,
time spent waiting for data exceeded time spent computing on it by a factor of
eight to twenty; changing two loader settings moved GPU utilisation by 24 to 51
percentage points.

The contribution is a replacement data loader that profiles its own behaviour
during a short warm-up, identifies which stage dominates, and selects one of
three strategies: read files individually as before, read them from tar archives
so that many small reads become few large ones, or copy them to fast node-local
disk before they are needed. The third is possible because the order in which
samples will be used is fixed at the start of each pass through the data. The
loader therefore knows exactly which files it will need and when, which makes
preparing them ahead of time a scheduling problem rather than a prediction
problem -- the distinction that separates this from an ordinary cache.

The evaluation produced one positive result and one negative one. The mechanisms
themselves work: reading from archives beat reading individual files on every
architecture tested, and copying the dataset to local disk in advance gave the
highest utilisation of any configuration for two of the three. The automatic
selection did not work: it almost always chose the plain strategy even where a
better one was available. The fault lay in the measurement rather than the
decision rule. The profiling window was only eight batches long -- short enough
that one-off GPU initialisation dominated the average and made computation appear
six to ten times slower than it really is, which in turn made the data path look
adequate by comparison. Discarding the first few batches before measuring
recovers an accurate reading at no meaningful cost.

The wider point is not specific to archaeology. On shared clusters the input
pipeline is frequently the limiting factor rather than the processor, and any
system that configures itself from its own measurements is only as reliable as
the window those measurements are taken over.
